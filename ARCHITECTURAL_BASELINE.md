# Architectural baseline

This is a small, revisable starting point for reading Korean palace architecture. It brings together useful ideas from inspected sources so that a later research or modeling session can begin with better questions. The books and articles remain individual sources, with their own subjects, methods, and limitations. Their inclusion here does not make their conclusions definitive.

The first version, seeded on 2026-09-21, draws on three supplied scholarly articles retained in Dropbox. It covers a structural example, the relationship between buildings and their setting, and the limits of transferring a comparative system. Building dimensions and historical states remain in the source and building records described in [DATA.md](DATA.md).

## Read the structure and the enclosure together

Guo's study of **Geunjeongjeon (근정전)** and **Injeongjeon (인정전)** provides an instructive distinction between exterior appearance and interior space. The article describes both halls as having two tiers of eaves while containing a single tall interior. Its Geunjeongjeon section shows inner and outer column networks, with tall inner supports and a different arrangement of supports around the edge. This gives us a concrete reason to read a section alongside an elevation before deciding where floors, ceilings, and roof supports belong. See [Guo 2005, note-001](sources/src-guo-joseon-timber-2005/source.json), PDF pages 6-7, printed pages 55-56, figures 4-5.

For our working vocabulary, a **column grid** describes the positions and intervals of supports; an **enclosure** describes the walls, partitions, and openings that bound space. Our practical inference is to inspect both: a grid alone does not establish every room boundary, and an exterior opening does not establish its operation or the space it serves. Record those relationships when the evidence provides them. These two monumental halls are useful teaching examples; residential rooms still need their own plans, sections, and construction evidence.

## Read a building in its setting

Kim Sung-Woo treats the relationship among buildings, walls, and courtyards as central to understanding Korean architectural beauty. His discussion makes the spaces between structures part of the subject, and relates architecture to lived experience. This is a useful interpretive perspective for a project concerned with how palace spaces connect. See [Kim 2007, note-001](sources/src-kim-korean-architecture-beauty-2007/source.json), PDF page 4, printed pages 40-41.

Our practical use of this argument is to retain setting and relationships when collecting evidence: which courtyard an opening faces, how a wall defines an approach, and how adjacent spaces connect. Kim's broad contrast between Korean and Western architecture remains his argument. His conclusion acknowledges the difficulty of giving a clear account of Korean architectural beauty (PDF page 20, printed pages 72-73); this baseline does not turn that argument into a universal historical rule.

## Let comparisons sharpen questions

Guo's article on the *Yingzao Fashi* explains the manual's eight-grade **cai-fen** system in the context of Song Chinese building standards. The supplied file is a modern study of that manual. It helps us understand what an explicitly described system of timber sizes and proportions can look like. Its applicability to a particular Joseon building would require separate evidence. See [Guo 1998, note-001](sources/src-guo-yingzao-fashi-1998/source.json), PDF pages 7-8, printed pages 6-7, figure 2.

Even the directly relevant Korean case study leaves room for investigation. Guo discusses unequal bay spans within Geunjeongjeon, proposes a possible module, and calls for further research into Korean timber grading. Preserve the difference between the reported arrangement and the author's proposed explanation. See [Guo 2005, note-002](sources/src-guo-joseon-timber-2005/source.json), PDF pages 15-16, printed pages 64-65. Repeated forms can prompt a comparison; usable dimensions and construction rules need evidence for the building and state being studied.

## Date the assembly and its contents separately

The Sajeongjeon sources show why a room needs more than one date. Park describes a removable mural support, conservation work and the replacement of the painted original with a photographic facsimile in 2000. Her proposed date for the support differs from her argument about the painting's date. The official 2024 photographs document another layer: a newly reconstructed furnishing display. See [Park 2022, notes 001–003](sources/src-park-sajeongjeon-mural-2022/source.json), especially PDF pages 12–13 and 32, and the [2024 display record, notes 001–003](sources/src-gbg-sajeongjeon-furnishing-2024/source.json). These are source readings, with the historical arguments still subject to checking.

Our practical lesson is to track the structural opening, inserted support, painted surface and movable furnishings separately when their histories differ. A recent photograph may help locate an assembly while showing replacement contents or a visitor-viewing configuration. The [1993 drawing comparison, note-002](sources/src-gbg-sajeongjeon-measured-drawings-1993/source.json) adds a related distinction: measured and repair drawings can share a project date while serving different purposes. Keep their evidence separate before deciding which state to reconstruct.

## A repeated roofline can enclose different kinds of space

A palace range is not automatically an open cloister. Inspected Sajeongjeon and Geunjeongjeon descriptions distinguish storage, rooms, kitchens, maru and open circulation, sometimes with access from the opposite precinct. The dated 1395 account also distinguishes perimeter ranges from connecting passages. Record enclosure and connection endpoints, not only repeated bays; see [comparison C-003](COMPARISONS.md#c-003-ranges-and-connectors-are-spatially-different).

Residential descriptions likewise connect maru and ondol while preserving different peripheral spaces and platforms. Gyotaejeon's absence of a projecting 월대 does not imply absence of its building base. Shared vocabulary should help compare relationships without erasing such variants; see [comparison C-002](COMPARISONS.md#c-002-residential-similarity-does-not-erase-variants) and the [living briefs](typologies/README.md).

## What the next evidence should add

The [first Sajeongjeon observations](buildings/gbg-sajeongjeon.json) connect its column arrangement, numbered openings and northern interior frame across the plan and sections. The transcribed bay chains still need confirmed units and measuring heights, and one chain conflicts with its printed total. Korean terminology should grow from inspected labels, with a short explanation and a source example. Initial residential source notes now establish useful floor/room relationships, but floor assemblies, precise partitions, door operation and changes between documented states still need drawing-level checks. The retained sources do not supply a completed residential reconstruction.

For the Korean SNS audience, the project aims to make particular buildings and spaces recognizable and worth understanding. Specific evidence about their form, use, materials, and setting will strengthen that presentation. The visual guide is deferred while this base develops; art direction remains a later responsibility of [PalaceModeling](../PalaceModeling/README.md).

## Keep the baseline useful as the corpus grows

Update this document when inspected evidence changes a reusable explanation, resolves an ambiguity, or reveals a recurring mistake in our work. First record the finding and its locator in the appropriate source or building record, then revise the relevant paragraph here. Keep the scope and contrary evidence visible. A newer publication or an additional similar example does not automatically settle a disagreement.

Routine acquisition does not require a baseline update. Add only knowledge that helps a later session read, explain, or model the architecture; remove repetition as the document grows. An essay may offer a useful way of seeing, while a measured survey may answer a particular geometric question. Assess each source for the question at hand.

Any future skill should refer to this evolving baseline and teach a procedure we have actually used. Revise that procedure when experience warrants it, while keeping detailed architectural claims with their sources. For now, this document and the existing research workflow are the shared starting point.
